\section{Probabilistic State Estimation}
\label{sec:literature-review-probabilistic-state-estimation}
% TODO: Bayes filter, Kalman filter, EKF

As all sensors in the real world, the sensors used on the ASV are all noisy and do not return perfect measurements.
Physical/motion model approximations, environmental factors, actuations, give way to a great deal of uncertianty\citep[ch.~1]{thrun2005probabilistic}.
Due to all of these reasons, there are scenarios where some quantities of intereset can not be measured directly, only inferred from other measurements,
hence the need for a propabilistic representation of state\citep[ch.~2]{thrun2005probabilistic}.

A belief is a propability distribuition over the state, given all the measurements
and control inputs so far. The state is, in this case, the boats position and heading\citep[ch.~2]{thrun2005probabilistic}.
As all of these measurements carry uncertianty, the state can not be known exactly,
it should be described with a distribuition, where the spread of said distribuition
is the uncertianty.

The Bayes filter is the system in which these propability distribuitions are handled.
The filter works recursively, using only the newest control input and measurements.
It need not recalculate from the begginning, only the last step.
This recursive nature is made possible by the Markow assumption, which says:
Past and future data are independent if one know the current state.
What this means in this case is if the current state is known,
only the most recent belief matters in the prediction of the next state, as all control inputs and measurements, up to that moment,
have aleady been taken into account, only the newest measurements and control inputs are needed,
greatly decreasing computational costs\citep[ch.~2]{thrun2005probabilistic}.

There are two steps to the filter: Firstly the prediction step, then the correction step.
The predicition step propagates the previos belief through the motion model.
This takes the previos belief, control inputs, and the motion model, and calculates
the new predicted belief ($\overline{bel}(x_t)$).
The uncertianty grows with this step as, the motion model is imperfect.
Then the correction step weighs the predicted belief by the likelyhood of the
new measurement, then normalizes it. This step usually reduces the uncertianty\citep[ch.~2]{thrun2005probabilistic}.

\begin{align}
  bel(x_t) &= p(x_t | z_{1:t}, u_{1:t}) \\
  \overline{bel}(x_t) &= \int p(x_t | u_t, x_{t-1}) \, bel(x_{t-1}) \, dx_{t-1} \\
  bel(x_t) &= \eta \, p(z_t | x_t) \, \overline{bel}(x_t)
\end{align}

The above integrals of the Bayes filter, most of the time,
do not have a closed form solutions, so assumptions have to be made.
If the motion and measurement models are linear, and the initial belief and all noise is Gaussian,
the belief stays Gaussian at every step.
A Gaussian belief can be fully described by the mean and covariance~\citep[ch.~3]{thrun2005probabilistic}.

The Kalman filter~\citep{kalman1960new} is the Bayes filter for exactly this scenario.
Just like the Bayes filter it also works in two steps.
The prediction step moves the mean and grows covariance.
While the correction step pulls the mean towards the measurement and
shrinks the covariance.
Prediction and measurement are generally not equally reliable, so there is a need for
a weight. This weight is the Kalman gain ($K_t$). The Kalman gain has to be calculated
every step of the filter from the predicted covariance and measurement noise.
The Kalman gain is the optimal weighting under the above mentioned
linear-Gaussian assumptions~\citep[ch.~3]{thrun2005probabilistic}.

Below are the equations defining the Kalman filter, with the notation defined in Table~\ref{tab:kf-notation}.
The notation $R$ and $Q$ follow Thrun's convention.

\begin{align}
  \bar{\mu}_t &= A_t \mu_{t-1} + B_t u_t \\
  \bar{\Sigma}_t &= A_t \Sigma_{t-1} A_t^\top + R_t \\
  K_t &= \bar{\Sigma}_t C_t^\top \left( C_t \bar{\Sigma}_t C_t^\top + Q_t \right)^{-1} \\
  \mu_t &= \bar{\mu}_t + K_t \left( z_t - C_t \bar{\mu}_t \right) \\
  \Sigma_t &= (I - K_t C_t)\, \bar{\Sigma}_t
\end{align}

As discussed in the previous paragraph, the Kalman filter
only works with linear models and Guassian noise.
In the real world most of the models are nonlinear.
Like the ASV's motion/measurement model. These contain $\sin/\cos$ of the heading,
and buoys are measured with range and bearing (a square root and $\operatorname{atan2}$).
None of which are linear functions.
The main problem is that a Gaussian pushed through a nonlinear function loses
its Gaussian nature, hence application of the Kalman filter is non-feasable.
The main idea is to linearize these non-linear models. This is the idea the
Extended-Kalman filter is based on~\citep[ch.~3]{thrun2005probabilistic}.

The Extended-Kalman filter linearizes these non-linear models by utalizing
first-order Taylor expansions around the current estimate.
The filters $A$ and $C$ matrices become Jacobians: $G_t$ is the motion models
Jacobian with respect to the state, evaluated at the previous mean, and $H_t$
is the measurement model's Jacobian evaluated at the predicted mean.
The mean is still propagated through the non-linear models $g$ and $h$ functions.
Only the covariance and gain utalize the linearized
$G_t$ and $H_t$ Jacobians~\citep[ch.~3]{thrun2005probabilistic}.

\begin{align}
  \bar{\mu}_t &= g(u_t, \mu_{t-1}) \\
  \bar{\Sigma}_t &= G_t \Sigma_{t-1} G_t^\top + R_t \\
  K_t &= \bar{\Sigma}_t H_t^\top \left( H_t \bar{\Sigma}_t H_t^\top + Q_t \right)^{-1} \\
  \mu_t &= \bar{\mu}_t + K_t \left( z_t - h(\bar{\mu}_t) \right) \\
  \Sigma_t &= (I - K_t H_t)\, \bar{\Sigma}_t
\end{align}
